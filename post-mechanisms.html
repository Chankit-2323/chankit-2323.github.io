Mechanisms of Beta-Lactam Resistance in Gram-Negative Strains

Date: July 2026 | Author: Chankit | Fields: Microbiology, Molecular Genomics

As antimicrobial resistance accelerates, understanding how pathogens evade modern therapeutics is critical. This analytical overview highlights the primary biochemical pathways utilized by Gram-negative bacteria to resist beta-lactam class antibiotics.

1. Enzymatic Deactivation (Beta-Lactamases)

The most prevalent clinical resistance mechanism remains the enzymatic production of beta-lactamases (such as ESBLs and carbapenemases). These active enzymes structurally hydrolyze the central four-atom beta-lactam ring, completely rendering the antibiotic molecule ineffective before it ever reaches its target Penicillin-Binding Proteins (PBPs).

2. Porin Loss and Decreased Outer Membrane Permeability

Gram-negative bacteria possess a highly selective asymmetric lipid outer membrane. Mutations that structurally modify or completely downregulate porin channels (such as OmpF/OmpC alternatives) physically restrict hydrophilic antibiotic molecules from passing into the periplasmic space.

3. Multidrug Efflux Pump Overexpression

When antibiotic molecules successfully infiltrate the periplasm, macromolecular efflux assemblies (like the AcrAB-TolC tripartite system) actively pump toxic compounds back into the extracellular environment. This process drastically drops the intracellular therapeutic concentration below effective bactericidal thresholds.
