Prescribing in the Dark: How Empirical Antibiotic Use Fueling the AMR Crisis

In modern medicine, antibiotics are routinely treated as a first line of defense rather than a precision weapon. Daily, thousands of patients walk out of clinics with broad-spectrum antibiotic prescriptions without undergoing any clinical or microbiological diagnostic confirmation. This practice of "blind prescribing" bypassing the tracking of specific pathogens and their Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations (MIC) directly accelerates global antimicrobial resistance (AMR).

The Core Issue: Treating an unknown bacterial enemy with an unverified dosage creates a perfect evolutionary incubator for superbugs.

1. The Blind Spot: Missing Microbiological Confirmation

When a physician prescribes an antibiotic based purely on visual symptoms, they assume the underlying cause. Symptomatic presentation frequently overlaps across completely different pathogens. Treating a viral infection or a non-susceptible bacterial strain with standard antibiotics does nothing to clear the illness; instead, it exposes billions of innocent commensal microbes within the patient's gut microbiome to intense selective evolutionary pressure.

2. Ignoring the MIC: An Evolutionary Catalyst

The Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) is the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial agent that prevents visible growth of a microorganism in a laboratory setting. Prescribing without checking the MIC leads to dangerous dosing inaccuracies:

  • Under-dosing (Sub-lethal exposure): If the prescribed dosage falls below the true MIC of the target pathogen, it fails to kill the bacteria. Instead, it acts as an evolutionary training ground, allowing survivors to develop genetic mutations that protect against future treatments.
  • Over-dosing: Unnecessary toxic exposure that wipes out healthy microbiome networks, leaving room for hyper-virulent pathogens like Clostridioides difficile to dominate.

3. Broad-Spectrum Reliance vs. Target Stewardship

Because diagnostic workflows take 48 to 72 hours using legacy culture methods, clinicians lean heavily on heavy broad-spectrum agents. This scorched-earth policy eliminates highly vulnerable bacteria while leaving highly dangerous, resistant variants completely unchecked. True clinical stewardship requires narrow-spectrum alternatives tailored directly to confirmed microbiological profiles.

The Urgent Need for Rapid Diagnostics

To prevent global medicine from regressing into a pre-antibiotic era, healthcare ecosystems must pivot toward rapid point-of-care molecular assays and genomics tracking tools. Clinicians must be equipped to transition from empirical guessing patterns to diagnostic-driven precision therapies.

🧫 Manikomycin: A Hidden Antibiotic That Could Shape the Future of AMR Research

Blog by Chankit Giri
Research Scholar | CSIR–Institute of Microbial Technology (CSIR-IMTECH), Chandigarh, India

Original Research: A natural depsipeptide antibiotic binds the E-site of the bacterial ribosome (Nature, 2026) by Kaur M., Travin D., Jangra M., Zgurskaya H. I., Vázquez Laslop N., Wilson D. N., et al.

This article is an independent scientific summary written by Chankit Giri to communicate the findings of the published research. All scientific credit belongs exclusively to the original authors.

🌍 Why This Discovery Deserves Attention

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as one of the most serious threats to global health. According to the World Health Organization, bacterial pathogens are evolving resistance faster than new antibiotics are entering clinical practice. Routine surgeries, cancer chemotherapy, neonatal care, organ transplantation and intensive care medicine all depend upon antibiotics that remain effective. Unfortunately, the antibiotic discovery pipeline has slowed considerably over the last three decades.

Against this backdrop, researchers continue searching for completely new molecules that attack bacteria in different ways. Instead of modifying existing antibiotics, scientists are increasingly exploring natural products hidden within microorganisms. One such discovery is manikomycin, a natural antibiotic recently described in a landmark publication in Nature (2026).

Although manikomycin is still years away from becoming a medicine, its discovery has attracted worldwide attention because it interacts with bacteria through a previously unexplored mechanism. This finding demonstrates that nature continues to surprise scientists, even within microorganisms that have been investigated for decades.

"Sometimes the most valuable discoveries are hidden inside organisms we believed we already understood."

👩‍🔬 The Scientists Behind the Discovery

The research was carried out by an international team of microbiologists, structural biologists and medicinal chemists. Among the leading contributors is Dr. Nora Vázquez Laslop from the University of Illinois Chicago (UIC). Working together with collaborators across multiple research institutions, the team identified, isolated and characterized a previously unknown natural antibiotic from the soil bacterium Streptomyces rimosus.

Their work combined modern microbiology, high-resolution structural biology, chemical analysis, and ribosome biology to understand not only what this molecule is, but also how it inhibits bacterial growth. The study was subsequently published in Nature, one of the world's leading scientific journals.

🧫 What Is Manikomycin?

Manikomycin is a newly characterized natural depsipeptide antibiotic produced by Streptomyces rimosus, a soil-dwelling bacterium already famous for producing oxytetracycline.

Interestingly, Streptomyces rimosus has been studied by microbiologists for more than seventy years. Scientists believed that nearly all of its antibiotic-producing capabilities had already been discovered. However, using advanced analytical techniques, the research team identified a previously hidden metabolite that had escaped detection because it is naturally produced in extremely small amounts.

This discovery serves as an important reminder that even microorganisms investigated for decades may still contain chemically unique molecules capable of inspiring future medicines.

🔬 What Makes Manikomycin Different?

Many antibiotics used in modern medicine target the bacterial ribosome, the molecular machine responsible for synthesizing proteins. Drugs such as tetracyclines, macrolides, aminoglycosides, oxazolidinones, and chloramphenicol inhibit protein synthesis by binding to well-characterized regions of the ribosome.

The challenge is that bacteria have spent decades evolving resistance against these antibiotic classes. Mutations in ribosomal RNA, enzymatic modification of antibiotics, active efflux pumps, and reduced membrane permeability have collectively reduced the effectiveness of many clinically important drugs.

Manikomycin offers something different.

Using high-resolution cryo-electron microscopy, the researchers demonstrated that manikomycin binds to the E-site (Exit site) of the bacterial large ribosomal subunit. According to the authors, this region has not previously been exploited by approved antibiotics.

Instead of competing with existing ribosome-targeting antibiotics, manikomycin interacts with a distinct structural pocket, providing researchers with an entirely new framework for designing future antibacterial compounds.

🧬 Understanding the Ribosomal E-site

During bacterial protein synthesis, transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules move sequentially through three ribosomal binding sites:

  • A-site (Aminoacyl site): Incoming tRNA carrying amino acids enters the ribosome.
  • P-site (Peptidyl site): The growing protein chain is extended through peptide bond formation.
  • E-site (Exit site): Empty tRNA molecules leave the ribosome after delivering their amino acid.

The Nature study demonstrates that manikomycin binds near the E-site and interferes with normal ribosomal function. As a result, bacterial protein synthesis is disrupted, preventing continued bacterial growth.

Why is this important?

Because no currently approved antibiotic targets this region, the discovery opens a completely new direction for antibiotic research and drug design.

⭐ Why This Discovery Matters

Although manikomycin is still a research molecule rather than an approved medicine, its discovery is scientifically significant for several reasons.

  • It reveals a previously untargeted ribosomal binding site.
  • It introduces a completely new antibacterial mechanism.
  • It expands the diversity of known natural antibiotics.
  • It demonstrates that well-studied microorganisms still contain undiscovered bioactive compounds.
  • It provides medicinal chemists with a promising scaffold for designing improved derivatives.

Importantly, the discovery should not be interpreted as meaning bacteria cannot develop resistance against manikomycin in the future. Instead, it provides researchers with a valuable starting point for developing antibiotics that operate differently from existing drugs.

🌍 Implications for Antimicrobial Resistance Research

Antimicrobial resistance continues to increase worldwide because bacteria constantly evolve under selective pressure created by antibiotic exposure.

Many multidrug-resistant pathogens—including Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, and vancomycin-resistant enterococci—have acquired multiple mechanisms that reduce antibiotic susceptibility.

One long-term strategy for overcoming resistance is to identify completely new bacterial targets rather than repeatedly modifying existing antibiotics.

In this context, manikomycin represents more than just another antibiotic candidate; it illustrates how modern microbiology, structural biology, and natural-product chemistry can uncover previously hidden opportunities for drug discovery.

⚠️ Is Manikomycin Ready for Patients?

No.

The discovery reported in Nature represents an important scientific advance, but manikomycin is not yet approved for clinical use.

The researchers note that further work is required to improve its stability, pharmacological properties, toxicity profile, and overall suitability for therapeutic development.

Like many newly discovered natural products, it should currently be regarded as a lead compound rather than a finished drug.

🌱 Looking Ahead

Perhaps the most inspiring aspect of this study is the broader lesson it teaches.

Scientists often assume that microorganisms investigated for decades have already revealed all of their secrets. The discovery of manikomycin challenges that assumption and highlights the remarkable chemical diversity that still exists within nature.

With advances in genome mining, metabolomics, cryo-electron microscopy, artificial intelligence, and synthetic biology, researchers are now better equipped than ever to uncover hidden antibiotics that were previously impossible to detect.

As antimicrobial resistance continues to threaten global healthcare, discoveries like manikomycin provide renewed optimism that innovative science can still reveal powerful solutions from the microbial world.

💭 Final Thoughts

The discovery of manikomycin is an excellent example of curiosity-driven science leading to meaningful advances in antibiotic research.

Although much work remains before this molecule could become a therapeutic agent, identifying a previously untargeted ribosomal binding site significantly expands our understanding of bacterial biology and antibiotic mechanisms.

Every new antibacterial target strengthens the fight against antimicrobial resistance. Whether manikomycin itself reaches the clinic or simply inspires the next generation of antibiotics, its discovery represents an important milestone in modern microbiology.
